Classifying Heartbeats from ECG Signals: A Deep Neural Network Built & Trained from Scratch in PyTorch

Evaluating cardiac arrhythmias from first principles with pure tensor math, hand-derived backpropagation gradients, custom numerical optimizers, and a critical look at why balanced accuracy matters over raw accuracy on imbalanced clinical datasets.

1. Executive Summary & The Clinical Challenge

The human heart is an autonomous, electromechanically synchronized pump. Every heartbeat is initiated by a wave of electrical depolarisation originating at the sinoatrial (SA) node, propagating sequentially through the atria, the atrioventricular (AV) node, the bundle of His, and the Purkinje network into the ventricles. An Electrocardiogram (ECG) records these microvolt-to-millivolt surface potentials across time, generating the signature P-QRS-T waveform: 

       R (Ventricular Depolarisation)
       /\
      /  \
 P   /    \       T (Ventricular Repolarisation)
/\  /      \     /\
--\/--------\---/--\------ Isoelectric Baseline
  Q          S
  • P Wave: Atrial depolarisation initiating atrial contraction.
  • QRS Complex: Rapid ventricular depolarisation triggering powerful mechanical ventricular ejection.
  • T Wave: Ventricular repolarisation restoring resting electrical equilibrium.

When structural myocardial disease, localized ischemia, or ectopic pacemaker foci disrupt this electrical circuit, the heart enters an arrhythmia. Premature Ventricular Contractions (PVCs) can trigger catastrophic ventricular tachycardia or fatal ventricular fibrillation within seconds. Continuous automated detection in intensive care units (ICUs) and wearable holter monitors is life-critical.

In this project, we formulate and address a foundational engineering question:

"How well does a small neural network recognise each distinct type of heartbeat, and is overall accuracy the right number to trust when healthy beats heavily dominate clinical datasets?"

To answer this rigorously, we design, derive, train, and validate a 3-layer deep neural network (ECGNet) completely from scratch in PyTorch—enforcing a strict zero-abstraction constraint (no nn.Linear, no nn.Sequential, no nn.ReLU, no torch.optim, and no high-level loss functions).

The Core Dilemma: Raw Accuracy vs. Balanced Accuracy

In our holdout test set of 21,892 real patient heartbeats from the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database, 18,118 beats (82.76%) are Normal, while life-threatening Ventricular Ectopic beats make up only 6.61%, and Fusion beats account for just 0.74%.

Consider a trivial "Always-Normal" baseline classifier that unconditionally predicts Class N for every incoming signal without performing any computation: 

Accuracy(Always-N Baseline) = 18,118 / 21,892 = 82.76%

On paper, 82.76% appears respectable. In an ICU setting, however, this baseline monitor would miss 100% of ventricular ectopic arrhythmias and 100% of supraventricular contractions, leading to fatal clinical consequences. To evaluate real clinical discrimination, we compute both Overall Accuracy and Balanced Accuracy (the unweighted mean of per-class sensitivities): 

Accuracy = (1 / m) Σ 1[y_hat == y]
Balanced Accuracy = (1 / 5) Σ [ Accuracy(c) ]  for c ∈ {N, S, V, F, Q}

Our from-scratch network achieves 97.82% overall test accuracy and 83.65% balanced accuracy, delivering robust true positive sensitivity across rare pathological beats.

2. Dataset, Splitting Protocol & Morphological Anatomy

We utilized the standard MIT-BIH Arrhythmia benchmark dataset curated by Shayan Fazeli on Kaggle. Each heartbeat is segmented around the R-peak, resampled to fs = 125 Hz, zero-padded to a uniform vector length of 187 temporal voltage samples (∼1.5 seconds), and normalized between 0.0 and 1.0.

Morphological Waveform Anatomy:

  • Class N (Normal Beat): Sharp, narrow QRS complex preceded by a well-defined P wave and followed by an isoelectric ST segment and positive T wave.
  • Class S (Supraventricular Ectopic): Originates above the ventricles (atria or AV node). Because depolarisation travels through the normal His-Purkinje system, its QRS duration is almost normal; distinguishing S from N hinges on premature timing and subtle P-wave distortions.
  • Class V (Ventricular Ectopic / PVC): Originates directly in ventricular myocardium. Conduction crawls through slow muscle-to-muscle pathways, creating a wide, bizarre, high-amplitude, often biphasic QRS complex with discordant T waves.
  • Class F (Fusion Beat): Simultaneous ventricular depolarisation from both the normal supraventricular pathway and an ectopic ventricular pacemaker, yielding an intermediate hybrid waveform.
  • Class Q (Unknown / Paced Beat): Characterized by sharp, high-frequency artificial pacemaker spikes followed by delayed, wide ventricular depolarisation.

Strict Deterministic Splitting Protocol:

The training file of 87,554 rows was partitioned into 90% Training (78,799 rows) and 10% Validation (8,755 rows) using a fixed pseudorandom seed (np.random.default_rng(42)). The independent test dataset (21,892 rows) remained completely sealed throughout model architecture selection, hyperparameter sweeps, and epoch tuning.

3. Network Topology & Parameter Count Derivation

To examine deep learning mechanics without framework shielding, our architecture (ECGNet) relies exclusively on raw 2D tensor weight matrices and 1D bias vectors registered as nn.Parameter.

Layer Dimensions & Mathematical Forward Pass:

  • Input Dimension (n[0]): 187 temporal voltage points.
  • Hidden Layer 1 (n[1]): 128 units, activated by custom clamped ReLU: A[1] = clamp(X W[1]T + 1B b[1]T, min=0)
  • Hidden Layer 2 (n[2]): 64 units, activated by custom clamped ReLU: A[2] = clamp(A[1] W[2]T + 1B b[2]T, min=0)
  • Output Layer (n[3]): 5 linear logits corresponding to classes {N, S, V, F, Q}: Z[3] = A[2] W[3]T + 1B b[3]T

He (Kaiming) Normal Weight Initialization:

All weight matrices are initialized from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution with standard deviation scaled by the input fan-in: 

W[ℓ]ij ~ N(0, σ = sqrt(2 / n[ℓ-1])),    b[ℓ] = 0

Why is this mathematically essential?

  • Symmetry Breaking: Initializing weights to zero causes every hidden unit to compute identical activations (z = 0) and receive identical backpropagated gradients. The hidden layer acts as a single scalar neuron, permanently bottlenecking representational capacity.
  • Variance Preservation under ReLU: Standard Xavier initialization (σ2 = 1/nin) assumes linear activations. ReLU zeroes out all negative activations (∼50% of incoming signals), cutting activation variance in half. He initialization doubles the variance (σ2 = 2/nin), ensuring constant signal power and preventing gradient decay across feedforward layers.

4. Numerically Stable Loss & Analytical Gradient Verification

For a mini-batch matrix of logits Z ∈ ℝB × 5 and ground-truth targets y ∈ {0, …, 4}B, naive evaluation of cross-entropy via exp(z) can cause arithmetic overflow in 32-bit floating point when z > 88. We implemented numerically stable softmax cross-entropy using the LogSumExp trick: 

log Σ exp(z_j) = c + log Σ exp(z_j - c),    where c = max_j(z_j)
J = (1 / B) Σ [ -Z[3]i, y_i + logsumexp(Z[3]i, :) ]

Initial Loss Theoretical Sanity Check: At step 0 with random Gaussian weights and zero biases, each of the 5 classes is approximately equiprobable (P ≈ 0.20): 

E[Jinit] ≈ -ln(1 / 5) = ln(5) ≈ 1.609438

Our measured empirical initial loss before training was 1.741343, providing immediate mathematical confirmation of correct loss scaling.

Analytical Backpropagation Gradient Verification:

To verify PyTorch's automatic differentiation engine and prove our forward implementation, we derived the Layer 3 analytical gradients by hand using vector calculus: 

dJ / dZ[3] = (1 / B) (Y_hat - Y)
dJ / dW[3] = (1 / B) (Y_hat - Y)T A[2]
dJ / db[3] = (1 / B) Σ (y_hat(i) - y(i))

5. Custom Optimizer & Learning Rate Dynamics

Rather than relying on torch.optim.SGD, we engineered a custom parameter update engine featuring Polyak Momentum (β = 0.9) and L2 Weight Decay (λ = 10-4): 

# Gradient computation with weight decay (weights only)
g_P = dJ / dP + λ · P   (if P is weight)
g_P = dJ / dP             (if P is bias)

# Momentum velocity state accumulation
v_P := β · v_P + (1 - β) · g_P

# Parameter step
P := P - α · v_P

Learning Rate Sweep (α ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 0.5}):

We evaluated three learning rate candidates over 30 epochs with batch size B = 128 and full dataset epoch-level reshuffling:

Clinical Misclassification Analysis: Why are S and F Challenging?

  • Class S (66.37% Sensitivity): 181 out of 556 Supraventricular beats were misclassified as Normal (N). Because supraventricular premature depolarisations enter the ventricles through the intact AV node and bundle branches, the resulting ventricular depolarisation wave produces a narrow, normal-looking QRS complex. Distinguishing S from N relies predominantly on detecting subtle shifts in P-wave timing and amplitude.
  • Class F (62.96% Sensitivity): 39 out of 162 Fusion beats were misclassified as Normal, and 21 as Ventricular. By definition, a fusion beat is a physical hybrid produced when both normal sinus activation and an ectopic ventricular pacemaker activate cardiac tissue concurrently.
  • Bayesian Prior Bias: In the training data, Class N outnumbers Class F by 113 to 1 (65,224 vs 577). On morphologically borderline beats, the network's learned prior naturally favors predicting Class N unless feature evidence is overwhelming.

7. Deep Inquiries: Optimization & Regularization Dynamics

7.1 What does momentum do when consecutive gradients point in opposite directions?

The velocity update v := β v + (1 - β) g acts as an exponential low-pass filter. When consecutive mini-batch gradients point in opposing directions (common along steep ravines or canyon walls in the loss surface), the opposing terms cancel out (gt ≈ -gt-1 &implies; v ≈ 0). This drastically damps high-frequency transverse oscillations while reinforcing persistent gradient directions, stabilizing the optimizer.

7.2 What does weight decay do to a weight that receives zero gradient?

If a feature is completely uninformative or dormant such that ∂J / ∂W = 0, the effective gradient reduces purely to the regularisation term: g = λ W. Over successive parameter updates: 

W := W - α · (1 - β) · λ W = W · [ 1 - α · (1 - β) · λ ]

Because the multiplier is strictly less than 1, the weight decays exponentially toward zero with every mini-batch step. This prunes unneeded synapses, suppresses co-adaptation, and prevents overfitting.

7.3 How can we improve sensitivity on rare arrhythmias like Fusion (F)?

The most principled remedy is Class-Weighted Softmax Cross-Entropy: 

Jweighted = (1 / B) Σ wy_i · [ -Z[3]i, y_i + logsumexp(Z[3]i, :) ]
wc = Ntotal / (5 · Nc)  &implies;  wN ≈ 0.24,   wF ≈ 27.32

Under this weighting, a classification error on a rare Fusion beat penalizes the objective function 113 times more heavily than an error on a Normal beat, compelling the optimizer to carve out distinct decision boundaries around rare pathological morphologies.

9. Summary & Engineering Takeaways

  • Zero-Abstraction Mastery: Building neural networks directly from raw tensors, explicit parameter matrices, and manual gradient verification reveals foundational insights that high-level APIs abstract away.
  • Metric Integrity: In clinical biomedical ML, raw overall accuracy can be deceptively reassuring. Balanced accuracy (83.65% vs 97.82%) and confusion matrices are indispensable for evaluating real patient risk.

Detecting Faults on Three-Phase Transmission Lines: From First-Principles Logistic Regression to Support Vector Machines

A practical deep-dive into transmission line fault classification using custom NumPy implementations and Support Vector Machines (Linear vs. RBF Kernels).

1. Introduction & The Engineering Challenge

In high-voltage electrical power grids, three-phase transmission lines are the critical arteries carrying bulk electrical power across vast distances. However, these lines operate exposed to severe environmental stressors and are vulnerable to various fault conditions:

  • Line-to-Ground (L-G) Faults: Typically caused by lightning strikes, tree encroachment, or insulation flashover.
  • Line-to-Line (L-L) Faults: Physical contact between phase conductors due to high winds, conductor galloping, or tower sway.
  • Double Line-to-Ground (L-L-G) Faults: Multi-phase contact involving ground path.
  • Three-Phase Symmetrical (L-L-L / L-L-L-G) Faults: Severe short-circuits across all phases simultaneously.

When a short-circuit occurs, instantaneous currents surge to destructive levels, while voltages sag sharply due to the collapse of line impedance. A numerical protective relay must detect this abnormal condition within milliseconds (typically 1–2 cycles of 50/60 Hz power frequency, or 20–40 ms) and send a trip signal to the circuit breaker before transformer cores saturate or conductors melt.

In this project, we address a foundational engineering and machine learning question:

"Can a linear decision boundary detect a fault from instantaneous 3-phase current and voltage measurements? If not, what is the simplest and most effective fix: expanding the feature space (e.g., quadratic features) or using non-linear kernels (e.g., RBF Support Vector Machines)?"

To rigorously understand the underlying mathematical mechanics, we implement Logistic Regression completely from scratch using NumPy (avoiding high-level abstractions like Scikit-Learn's estimators, scalers, or train-test split utilities) and benchmark it directly against Linear and Radial Basis Function (RBF) Support Vector Machines (SVMs).

2. Dataset and Preprocessing Pipeline

We utilized the Electrical Fault Detection and Classification benchmark dataset from Kaggle, simulated in MATLAB/Simulink. Each observation represents an instantaneous measurement sample containing 6 physical quantities:

  • Ia, Ib, Ic: Instantaneous currents of phases A, B, and C (Amperes).
  • Va, Vb, Vc: Instantaneous voltages of phases A, B, and C (per-unit system).
  • y ∈ {0, 1}: Binary ground-truth target where 1 denotes a fault condition and 0 denotes normal healthy operation.

Data Cleaning and Leak-Free Standardisation:

  • Uninformative Column Removal: The raw CSV dataset contained two empty trailing columns (Unnamed: 7 and Unnamed: 8), which were dropped.
  • Deterministic Partitioning: The cleaned dataset (12,001 rows) was split into 60% Training (7,200 rows), 20% Validation (2,400 rows), and 20% Test (2,401 rows) using a fixed random seed (np.random.default_rng(42)). The fault class proportion remained balanced across all splits (~45.1% – 46.1% faults).
  • Strict Standardisation: To prevent data leakage, the mean μ and standard deviation σ were computed solely from the training partition and then applied to transform the validation and test partitions: z = (x - μtrain) / σtrain

Feature Sets Defined:

  • Set R (Raw Features, d=6): [Ia, Ib, Ic, Va, Vb, Vc]
  • Set Q (Quadratic Features, d=12): [Ia, Ib, Ic, Va, Vb, Vc, Ia2, Ib2, Ic2, Va2, Vb2, Vc2]

Crucial engineering detail: For Set Q, the squared features were computed from the raw physical values before computing the 12-dimensional training mean and standard deviation for standardisation.

3. Exploratory Data Analysis: Why Linear Classifiers Fail

Before training any machine learning model, exploratory data analysis reveals the fundamental physical geometry of power transmission signals.

In normal healthy conditions, currents follow clean sinusoidal oscillations with bounded amplitudes. Once a fault strikes, the currents surge instantaneously by several orders of magnitude, accompanied by severe phase asymmetry and voltage collapse.

The Phase-Plane Geometry (Ia vs Ib):

  • Normal operating points form a compact cluster centered at the origin (0, 0).
  • Fault points form a broad annular ring (torus) completely encircling the normal operating cluster.

Why does a linear hyperplane fail? Because alternating currents oscillate symmetrically across zero every half cycle, instantaneous fault currents swing between positive peaks and negative troughs. Fault states surround the origin in all directions. A flat linear hyperplane cutting through the 6D feature space cannot isolate the interior healthy core from the exterior surrounding fault ring without triggering immense misclassifications.

4. Model Implementation & Training

4.1 Logistic Regression from Scratch (NumPy)

The logistic regression model parameters θ ∈ ℝd+1 were trained using custom vectorized NumPy routines:

  • Sigmoid Hypothesis: hθ(x) = 1 / (1 + exp(-θT x))
  • Negative Log-Likelihood Cost with Numerical Clipping: Probabilities were clipped to [10-12, 1 - 10-12] to prevent ±∞ overflow during log evaluations.
  • Vectorized Gradient Descent: Parameters updated iteratively via θ := θ + (α/m) XT (y - hθ(X)).

For both feature sets, cost curves strictly monotonically decreased with α = 1.0 (Set R) and α = 10.0 (Set Q), converging cleanly from the theoretical initial cost J(0) = ln(2) ≈ 0.693147.

6.2 Support Vector Dynamics: Linear vs RBF

The Linear SVM required 5,835 support vectors out of 7,200 training rows (81.0%)! This enormous support vector count confirms that points are constantly violating the margins and that linear separation is fundamentally impossible.

In contrast, the RBF SVM with C=100 and γ=1.0 achieved 99.6% accuracy with only 266 support vectors (3.7%). The kernel function implicitly projects the data into an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space where an optimal maximum-margin hyperplane separates the fault manifolds effortlessly.

6.3 Relay Engineering: Missed Faults vs False Alarms

In protective relaying, a missed fault (False Negative) is an operational disaster. If a fault is missed, fault current continues to arc through the transmission tower, destroying transformers, endangering human life, and destabilizing the grid. A false alarm (False Positive) is merely an inconvenience that causes an unnecessary breaker trip.

While linear models miss over 630 faults on the test set, the RBF SVM reduces missed faults to just 6 (and Set Q Logistic Regression reduces it to 45).

8. Summary & Key Takeaways

  • Geometry Dictates Model Choice: AC oscillatory signals create circular/annular geometric distributions in phase-space that linear models cannot separate (capped at ~73%).
  • Physics-Informed Feature Engineering: Introducing quadratic features (I2, V2) lifted simple logistic regression accuracy from 73.0% to 97.9% without adding complex model latency.
  • Non-Linear Kernels: RBF SVM achieved the highest test performance (99.58% accuracy with only 6 missed faults).
  • Zero-Dependency Fundamentals: Writing logistic regression from scratch in pure NumPy provides profound insight into standardisation, numerical stability, and optimization dynamics.

Predicting Solar Power Plant Output from Weather Data: A Linear Regression Study from Scratch

Evaluating On-Site Weather Sensors vs. Public Satellite Reanalysis Data for Grid-Scale Solar Generation Forecasting

1. The Question & Problem Motivation

Solar photovoltaic (PV) power generation is inherently variable, dependent on diurnal solar geometry, atmospheric cloud attenuation, and panel temperature dynamics. For electrical grid operators, predicting solar plant AC power generation hours in advance is essential for load balancing, spinning reserve allocation, and grid frequency stability.

In this study, we investigate three core machine learning questions:

  • Predictive Accuracy: Can linear regression fit strictly from scratch accurately predict plant-level hourly AC power generation (PAC) using atmospheric weather variables?
  • On-Site vs. Remote Public Data: How much prediction accuracy is lost when replacing expensive on-site weather sensors with free, publicly accessible satellite reanalysis weather data (Open-Meteo API)?
  • Optimization Solvers: How do closed-form analytical solvers (Normal Equation) compare to iterative optimization algorithms (Batch Gradient Descent and Stochastic Gradient Descent)?

2. Data Engineering & Preparation

We analyze 34 days of continuous generation and weather data (May 15, 2020 – June 17, 2020) from Plant 1, located near Gandikota, Andhra Pradesh, India (Lat 14.82°N, Lon 78.28°E).

Preprocessing Pipeline:

  • Timestamp Standardization: Raw generation files used non-standard date strings (DD-MM-YYYY HH:MM), whereas weather sensors logged standard ISO timestamps. All timestamps were standardized into native UTC/IST datetimes.
  • Plant-Level Power Aggregation: The plant contains 22 separate central inverters. We aggregated total plant generation by summing AC_POWER and DC_POWER across all inverters at each 15-minute timestamp.
  • Hourly Resampling: The 15-minute merged generation and weather readings were resampled to hourly arithmetic means, producing a clean dataset of 796 hourly rows.

3. Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

Before modeling, we explored the physical relationships across solar generation and meteorological variables.

Key EDA Insights:

  • Linear Photovoltaic Response: AC power shows a strong linear correlation with solar irradiance (r > 0.95).
  • Thermal Panel Elevation: PV module temperature regularly exceeds ambient air temperature by 15–25°C under direct solar irradiance due to radiative heating.
  • Inverter Conversion Ratio: AC power vs DC power exhibits a near-perfect linear ratio of 0.098 (accounting for central inverter scaling and conversion efficiency).
  • Diurnal Bell Curve: Hourly mean AC power follows a symmetrical bell curve peaking between 11:00 AM and 1:00 PM, dropping to zero at night.

4. Location Verification: On-Site Sensor vs. Open-Meteo API

To verify the geographical coordinates of Plant 1 (Lat 14.82°N, Lon 78.28°E), we downloaded historical ERA5 reanalysis weather data from the Open-Meteo Historical Weather API and compared on-site sensor irradiance against satellite-derived shortwave radiation.

Verification Findings:

  • The correlation between on-site sensor irradiation and Open-Meteo shortwave radiation reached 0.9333, confirming exact spatial alignment.
  • Both signals reach peak intensity at 12:00 PM IST, confirming no time-zone offset or solar longitude discrepancy.

5. Linear Regression Implementation & Solver Results

All algorithms were implemented from scratch using NumPy matrix operations:

  • Normal Equation: θ = (XTX)-1 XTy
  • Batch Gradient Descent: θ ← θ − α (1/m) XT(Xθ − y)
  • Stochastic Gradient Descent: θ ← θ − α (x(i))T(x(i)θ − y(i))

Feature Sets:

  • Set A (On-Site Sensors): irradiation, module_temp, ambient_temp, sin/cos hour encoding (d=5)
  • Set B (Public Weather): sw_radiation, temp_2m, cloud_cover, sin/cos hour encoding (d=5)

7. Model Diagnostics & Residual Analysis

  • Nighttime (19:00 – 05:00): Residuals are identically zero due to post-processing ReLU clipping (max(ŷ, 0)).

Building an Autonomous Line-Following Robot

One of my fundamental projects during my B.S. in Electrical Engineering at FAST NUCES was designing and building a completely autonomous line-following robot from scratch. Hardware Architecture

At the brain of the operation was the Arduino Uno. I selected it for its rapid prototyping capabilities and robust community support. To detect the line, I implemented an array of IR (Infrared) sensors. These sensors constantly measure the reflectivity of the surface beneath them—differentiating between the dark track line and the lighter floor.

The motor control was handled via an L298N motor driver, enabling bidirectional control of two DC motors. Power management was critical; I had to ensure the motors drew enough current without browning out the microcontroller.

Software and PID Tuning

Writing the control logic was the most challenging and rewarding part. A simple "if left sensor sees black, turn left" algorithm results in incredibly jerky and inefficient movement. To solve this, I implemented a custom algorithm inspired by PID (Proportional, Integral, Derivative) control logic.

"The key to smooth robotics isn't just knowing where you are, but predicting where the momentum will take you in the next few milliseconds."

By reading the analog values of the IR array, the robot could calculate an "error" value based on how far off-center the line was, and adjust the motor PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) speeds proportionally. This resulted in buttery-smooth cornering.

Conclusion

This project solidified my understanding of closed-loop control systems and real-time microcontroller programming. It served as a fantastic stepping stone into more complex embedded architectures.

Simulating an OS: Process & Resource Management in User-Space

As an Electrical Engineer, my focus is primarily on hardware, but understanding how software interfaces with the CPU is crucial. To bridge this gap, I developed an OS Process and Resource Manager—a User-Space Operating System Simulator written entirely in C++.

Core Mechanisms Modeled

Operating systems are massive, complex beasts. For this simulator, I focused strictly on the core kernel responsibilities:

  • Process States: Implemented a state machine for processes transitioning between New, Ready, Running, Waiting, and Terminated.
  • Context Switching: Simulated saving and loading the CPU registers (Process Control Block - PCB) when swapping out processes.
  • Resource Allocation: Implemented Banker's Algorithm to handle simulated memory and peripheral requests, ensuring the system never enters a deadlock state.

Data Structures & Algorithms

Efficiency was key. I utilized custom queue structures to manage the Ready Queue and Waiting Queues. The scheduling algorithm utilized was a simulated Round-Robin with dynamic time-quantums, adjusting based on whether the process was I/O bound or CPU bound.

Practical Analog Automation: Proteus Circuit Simulations

Before soldering a single component, a robust circuit design must be simulated and stressed. I frequently use Proteus to validate my analog designs. Two of my favorite foundational analog projects are the Automatic Street Light System and the Temperature Controlled Fan.

The Automatic Street Light

This project entirely avoids microcontrollers. It relies purely on the physics of an LDR (Light Dependent Resistor) and the switching properties of BJT transistors. By setting up the LDR in a voltage divider configuration with a variable potentiometer, I created a tunable threshold. As ambient light drops, the LDR resistance spikes, altering the voltage at the transistor's base. Once the base-emitter voltage exceeds ~0.7V, the transistor saturates, triggering a relay that powers the street lamp. It's an elegant, highly reliable, and cheap analog solution.

The Temperature Controlled Fan

Similar in philosophy, this circuit utilizes an NTC Thermistor or an LM35 sensor. Using an Operational Amplifier (Op-Amp) configured as a comparator, the circuit compares the voltage from the temperature sensor against a reference voltage (set by a potentiometer).

When the temperature crosses the threshold, the Op-Amp outputs high, driving a MOSFET which in turn spins up a DC cooling fan.

These projects reinforce a core engineering philosophy: don't use a $5 microcontroller when a $0.10 Op-Amp and a sensor can do the job with zero latency and infinite reliability.
